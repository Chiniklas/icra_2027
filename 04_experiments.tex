\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

We evaluate each condition over $N=10$ trials, with up to ten cycles per trial.
Each cycle consists of a $60^\circ$ clockwise turn followed by release, return,
and re-engagement.

\paragraph{Maximum Cycle Count}
For trial $i$, $C_i\in[0,10]$ counts complete cycles before failure or timeout;
incomplete cycles are excluded. We report
$\bar C=N^{-1}\sum_i C_i$; higher is better.

\paragraph{Cyclic Tracking Error}
For completed cycle $k$ in trial $i$, the angular error is
$e_{i,k}=\Delta\theta_{i,k}-60^\circ$, where $\Delta\theta_{i,k}$ is the
clockwise nut rotation. Over all $M=\sum_i C_i$ completed cycles, we report
$\mu_{\mathrm{cyc}}=M^{-1}\sum_{i,k}e_{i,k}$ and standard deviation
$\sigma_{\mathrm{cyc}}$. Negative values indicate lag and positive values
overshoot; a mean closer to zero and a smaller deviation are better.

\paragraph{Ten-Cycle Tracking Error}
For trial $i$, ten-cycle error is
$e_{10,i}=\sum_{k=1}^{C_i}\Delta\theta_{i,k}-600^\circ$; incomplete cycles are
excluded. We report its trial mean, equivalently
$\mu_{10}=\bar C(60^\circ+\mu_{\mathrm{cyc}})-600^\circ$. If no cycle succeeds,
both error metrics are reported as ``--.'' All errors are in degrees and use
the sign convention above.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

We simulate a seven-DoF Franka Research~3 (FR3) arm equipped with an Inspire
Hand RH56DFX using Isaac Sim~5.1.0 and Isaac Lab~2.2.1. The 9-D action space
encodes a six-DoF Cartesian-controller target and three index--thumb joint
commands. At
\SI{15}{Hz},
the student converts a calibrated $320\times180$ depth image into 4096 cropped
points and fuses a 64-dimensional DP3 feature with proprioception, progress,
and phase; physics runs at \SI{120}{Hz}. For the retained M24 run, we train the
PPO teacher for 200 epochs in 512 environments. Using only 20 successful
ten-cycle demonstrations (19,486 frames) collected from 32 environments, we
increase trajectory diversity by applying 30 random arm impulses to each
demonstration. We train the flow-matching student for 300 epochs using
eight-step action chunks.

\subsection{Ablations}

To investigate the contribution of individual components in our framework, we
conduct ablation studies focusing on four key aspects: \textbf{(Q1)} whether a
unified multi-cycle student outperforms repeated execution of a single-cycle
policy, \textbf{(Q2)} the contribution of the DP3 point-cloud encoder,
\textbf{(Q3)} the effectiveness of flow matching relative to deterministic
behavior cloning with LSTM and Transformer backbones, and
\textbf{(Q4)} the benefit of augmenting simulation demonstrations with
human-corrected real-world trajectories during student distillation. Results
are averaged over 10 trials on the M24 hexagonal nut and reported in
\tabref{tab:m24_ablation}.

We compare six ablations under matched training and evaluation budgets,
changing only the component specified by each ablation.

\noindent\textbf{(a) Single-Cycle Student.}
We train on real-world-augmented single-cycle demonstrations and invoke the
resulting policy ten times, resetting its temporal context after each cycle.

\noindent\textbf{(b) Ours + ResNet Visual Encoder.}
We replace DP3 with a one-branch, ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 stage-2 encoder
that fuses RGB, log depth, validity, and calibrated XYZ geometry into a 64-D
feature, while keeping the policy, objective, data, and training budget fixed.

\noindent\textbf{(c) DP3 Vision + LSTM Distillation.}
Using the same real-world-augmented dataset as ours, we replace the
flow-matching Transformer with the DEXTRAH-style backbone---a layer-normalized
512-unit LSTM followed by a 512--512--256 ELU MLP---trained using deterministic
supervised action regression.

\noindent\textbf{(d) DP3 Vision + Transformer Distillation.}
The deterministic Transformer has four layers, eight attention heads, a 512-D
hidden representation, 2048-D feed-forward layers, and eight learned queries
that predict an eight-step action chunk.

\noindent\textbf{(e) Ours with Only Simulated Data.}
We train the multi-cycle student using only simulated hybrid-teacher
demonstrations, excluding all human-corrected real-world data.

\noindent\textbf{(f) Ours.}
We train one flow-matching student on complete multi-cycle hybrid-teacher
demonstrations augmented with human-corrected real-world data.

To address \textbf{Q1}, we compare the single-cycle student (a) with our
multi-cycle student (f). Our full method completes all 10 cycles in both
simulation and the real world, whereas the single-cycle student completes only
5.5 and 5.2 cycles on average, respectively. A typical failure mode is that the
single-cycle-trained student slips off the edge of the nut after a few cycles,
showing that (a) learns the basic threading skill but fails to follow the axial
motion of the nut (\tabref{tab:m24_ablation}).

For \textbf{Q2}, we perform an ablation on the vision encoder using both DP3 and
ResNet-18 backbones, while holding the policy, objective, data, and training
budget fixed. A shared decoder is used to evaluate the prediction error of the
two encoded features. The DP3 encoder achieves a positional error of
\SI{8}{\milli\meter} and a rotational error of $15.16^\circ$ in simulation,
while ResNet-18 achieves a similar positional error of \SI{8.6}{\milli\meter}
but a notably higher rotational error of $31.20^\circ$. Real-world conditions
further amplify the difference, with ablation (f) completing all 10 cycles
while ablation (b) completes only 1.1 cycles on average, which we presume
results from overfitting to the first-cycle data (\tabref{tab:m24_ablation}).

Results from ablations (c), (d), and (f) show that the choice of student action
backbone makes a substantial difference in this long-horizon task, answering
\textbf{Q3}. While the LSTM backbone fails completely in simulation, diverging
before completing even the first cycle, the Transformer backbone survives
slightly longer and completes 1.4 cycles on average, but soon faces the same
compounding-error problem. Neither ablation (c) nor (d) advances to real-world
testing (\tabref{tab:m24_ablation}).

The comparison between ablations (e) and (f) provides strong evidence for
\textbf{Q4}. As shown in \tabref{tab:m24_ablation}, the student policy distilled
using only simulation data performs perfectly in Isaac Sim, completing all 10
cycles and even achieving a lower cyclic tracking error and standard deviation
than our full method. However, when deployed in the real world, the hardware
sim-to-real gap greatly affects its threading success rate, leaving ablation
(e) able to complete only 2.1 cycles on average, with a cyclic tracking error of
$-10.05^\circ$ and a standard deviation of $4.08^\circ$. In contrast, our full
method maintains consistent performance in both simulation and real-world
tests, completing all 10 cycles. In the real world, it achieves a cyclic
tracking error of $-4.21^\circ$ with a standard deviation of $1.20^\circ$,
slightly higher than in simulation.

In \tabref{tab:id_ood_comparison}, we provide test data for the in-distribution
M24, M30, and M36 hexagonal nuts, as well as the out-of-distribution octagonal,
rectangular, and round nuts, to evaluate the generalization ability of our
method. The results show that our method can execute the task on the
in-distribution training objects with impressive accuracy. Notably, the M30 nut
achieves the best performance, with an average lag of $3.7^\circ$ per cycle and
$37^\circ$ over the ten-cycle trial. The M36 nut achieves the worst performance
due to its larger size, mainly because the sim-to-real gap is amplified by the
penetration depth in the training environment, making deployment more
sensitive.

Across the tested out-of-distribution nut shapes, interestingly, the round nut
achieves the best performance, followed by the octagonal nut, in terms of both
mean cyclic tracking error and ten-cycle tracking error. These two nuts even
outperform the in-distribution M36 nut. The round nut's performance is likely
due to our unique zigzagged-rim design, which enables smoother force
transmission. The octagonal nut's performance is likely due to its similarity
to the hexagonal nut, further indicating that our nut-turning strategy works
more effectively with rounder shapes.

In contrast, the rectangular nut barely works with the currently trained
policy. A common failure mode occurs after the nut is turned $60^\circ$
clockwise: in the next cycle, the fingers are unable to establish contact with
the next pair of faces due to the rectangular shape, causing the nut to become
stuck at the first $60^\circ$ position. A smaller nut such as the M24 can escape
this failure mode by chance, but it survives for only another two cycles before
the mismatched policy completely fails.
