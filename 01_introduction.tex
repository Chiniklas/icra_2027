Threading a nut onto a bolt is among the most difficult tasks in robotic
assembly because threaded engagement is notoriously contact-rich. On the one
hand, the engaged threads can produce more than 15,000 simultaneous contact
points, making faithful simulation prohibitively expensive for the large number
of parallel rollouts required for reinforcement learning.
Factory\cite{narang2022factory}
directly addresses this computational bottleneck through a combination of
signed-distance-field (SDF)\cite{macklin2020local} collision detection, contact reduction, and a
Gauss--Seidel solver, enabling 1,000 parallel nut--bolt assemblies to be
simulated in real time on a single GPU.

On the other hand, depending on the task, humans perform nut threading in
various ways. For simple spinning, a single finger is sufficient while the
wrist remains fixed. A pinch grasp is preferred for controlled threading,
whereas a tripod or even a power grasp may be applied in high-resistance
scenarios.

Prior work has typically approached the problem from the perspective of
spinning. DexScrew\cite{dexscrew} uses a dexterous hand--arm setup similar to ours and replaces
explicit thread geometry with a revolute-joint proxy to learn a rotational
finger gait. The learned skill then assists teleoperation and supports the
collection of real-world demonstrations for tactile behavior cloning. However,
its policy performs uncontrolled spinning
without target conditioning or learned termination, leaving goal-directed,
cyclic nut threading unresolved.

Play2Perfect\cite{play2perfect} builds on the goal-conditioned
object-manipulation policy introduced by SimToolReal\cite{simtoolreal}. It
pretrains this policy through play on diverse objects and goals, then fine-tunes
it for precise assembly tasks, including screwing a rod-like furniture leg.
This formulation enables goal-directed screwing in a structured setting but
does not address everyday threaded objects, such as nuts and bolts.

We first attempted to approach the controlled threading problem for nuts and bolts
by training a unified teacher policy for the complete
multi-cycle task. In practice, however, designing rewards that capture both
long-horizon progress and precise transitions between phases proved exceedingly
difficult.

This motivates our central design principle: \textbf{decompose during data
generation, unify during deployment}. During data generation, a learned policy handles
approach, grasp, rotate, and goal reaching, while scripted transition
controllers handle release-and-reset behaviors. We refer to the
composition of the learned RL skill and scripted transition controllers as the
hybrid teacher, while human intervention is used only during real-world replay
to correct teacher trajectories under sim-to-real discrepancies. During
deployment, a student policy distills these heterogeneous demonstrations into
a single visuomotor policy that executes the complete cyclic task without
privileged state, scripted controllers, or human intervention. We evaluate
threading accuracy in both simulation and real-world experiments.

The main contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A long-horizon cyclic nut-threading formulation and hybrid teacher that
    combines an RL policy for approach, grasp, rotation, and
    goal reaching with scripted release-and-reset behaviors, generating coherent
    long-horizon, multi-cycle demonstrations without a monolithic RL teacher.

  \item A human-corrected sim-to-real demonstration strategy that selectively
    replaces failure-prone segments during real-world replay, adapting
    simulated behaviors to hardware contact and perception discrepancies.

  \item A unified 3D flow-matching visuomotor student that distills
    heterogeneous demonstrations into complete cyclic behavior without
    privileged state, scripted controllers, or human assistance at deployment.
    In real-world tests, it completes every 10-cycle trial on all training
    sizes and geometrically compatible unseen round and octagonal profiles,
    while exposing failure on the tested rectangular profiles.
\end{itemize}
