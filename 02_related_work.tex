\textbf{Contact-Rich Dexterous Manipulation and Sim-to-Real Transfer.}
Precise, contact-rich assembly requires closed-loop correction under uncertain
and only partially observed contact. A now-standard recipe learns force-aware
policies in simulation and transfers them to hardware through dynamics
randomization~\cite{tobin2017domain}. Factory~\cite{narang2022factory} makes
this tractable for tight-clearance assembly by simulating contact with discrete,
voxel-based signed-distance fields (SDFs) and a contact-reduction scheme,
providing the fast, stable interaction dynamics that force-based policies
depend on. Building on such simulators, FORGE~\cite{noseworthy2025forge}
conditions a policy on an interpretable force threshold and demonstrates robust
sim-to-real transfer on contact search, including single-stage nut threading,
under substantial pose uncertainty. Parallel efforts scale dexterous, vision-
based manipulation to multi-fingered hands and humanoids through automated
real-to-sim tuning and hybrid object representations~\cite{lin2025recipe},
object-centric goal-conditioned RL for zero-shot tool use~\cite{simtoolreal},
and large-scale visuomotor grasping~\cite{dextrahrgb}. A recurring obstacle,
however, is \emph{simulation exploitation}: when extended to dexterous assets,
residual inaccuracies in contact and friction modeling let a policy satisfy its
reward by exploiting non-physical simulator artifacts rather than learning
transferable behavior. Dexscrew~\cite{dexscrew} confronts this issue most
directly and is the closest prior work to ours: it learns nut-screwing skills
that remain effective despite imperfect simulation. It addresses the screwing
skill in isolation with a fixed hand, however, whereas \methodname{} targets
long-horizon, cyclic threading with a fully actuated multi-fingered dexterous
hand mounted on a repositioning arm, and we adopt Dexscrew as our primary point
of comparison. More broadly, \methodname{} inherits SDF-based contact simulation
and force-regulated interaction but deliberately restricts the teacher to a
\emph{simplified} simulation that preserves threading-relevant contact while
reducing the surface area for such exploitation, and it targets the
\emph{long-horizon, cyclic} regime rather than a single contact episode.

\textbf{Long-Horizon Manipulation and Policy Architecture.}
Extending short contact skills to long horizons exposes a fundamental
limitation of autoregressive policies: errors compound. Under closed-loop
execution the state distribution visited at test time drifts away from the
training distribution, and this covariate shift degrades performance
super-linearly with horizon length---the classical result motivating
interactive imitation learning~\cite{ross2011reduction}. Two broad strategies
respond to this challenge. The first decomposes the task and manages
transitions explicitly: Sequential Dexterity~\cite{chen2023sequential} chains
multiple dexterous RL sub-policies with a learned transition-feasibility
function that fine-tunes adjacent policies for compatible hand-off states and
enables autonomous switching for recovery. This modularity is powerful but
introduces multi-network training overhead, hand-crafted or learned transition
boundaries, and boundary failures at each hand-off---costs that recur every
cycle in a repetitive task. The second strategy retains a single model and
attacks drift at its source, for example by distilling simulator structure into
world models that support long-horizon planning and rapid real-world
adaptation~\cite{simdist}. \methodname{} follows the single-policy philosophy:
rather than learning a set of distinct sub-policies and a transition function
between them, we roll out one reactive policy inside a state machine that, upon
reaching a rotation threshold, executes a guided open-and-return to the initial
opened configuration and re-invokes the \emph{same} policy in the same
environment. This scripted repetition---not a learned inter-policy
transition---generates the long-horizon demonstrations, which we then compress
into a single continuous student policy, so no discrete policy switching occurs
at deployment.

\textbf{Policy Distillation and Generative Trajectory Modeling.}
Distilling a privileged teacher, trained with information unavailable on
hardware, into a perception-limited student is an established route to
sim-to-real transfer~\cite{chen2020cheating}. The expressiveness of the student
determines how faithfully it can reproduce complex, multi-modal expert
behavior. Diffusion policies model the action distribution as an iterative
denoising process and capture multi-modality that unimodal regression
misses~\cite{chi2023diffusion}, and 3D variants such as DP3 show that a compact
point-cloud representation yields sample-efficient, generalizable visuomotor
policies~\cite{ze2024dp3}. Flow matching~\cite{lipman2023flow} generalizes and
simplifies this family: it trains a continuous normalizing flow by regressing
the vector field of a conditional probability path, and, with
optimal-transport or rectified paths~\cite{liu2023rectified}, learns nearly
straight trajectories between noise and data that permit fast, few-step ODE
integration. These properties---multi-modal expressiveness, straight vector
fields, and efficient sampling---make flow matching an attractive backbone for
reactive robot policies, as recent vision-language-action models
demonstrate~\cite{black2024pi0}. For sim-to-real transfer specifically, the
choice of observation representation governs robustness to embodiment and
viewpoint shift~\cite{wilcox2025adapt3r}. \methodname{} unifies these threads:
we distill a privileged, force-aware simulation teacher into a flow-matching
student conditioned on a DP3 visual latent and force feedback, using the
straightness and multi-modality of the learned vector field to represent an
entire long-horizon cyclic trajectory distribution in a single reactive network.
