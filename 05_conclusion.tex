\subsection{Conclusion}

We presented \methodname, a framework for learning long-horizon cyclic nut
threading with dexterous hands. Rather than learning the entire task with a
monolithic policy, \methodname decomposes demonstration generation into an
RL-learned, goal-conditioned turning skill and scripted transition behaviors,
while distilling the resulting long-horizon trajectories into a unified
visuomotor policy for deployment. Human corrections during real-world replay
further adapt the demonstration distribution to contact and perception
discrepancies that are difficult to capture in simulation.

Experiments show
that the distilled policy reliably completes 10 consecutive threading cycles
on all training-distribution nut sizes and transfers to unseen round and
octagonal profiles, while rectangular profiles expose a limitation of the
learned re-grasp strategy. These results suggest that structured behavior
generation followed by unified policy distillation provides an effective
approach to long-horizon, contact-rich dexterous manipulation.

\subsection{Future Work}

Long-horizon stress tests reveal a clear limitation: the current student
policy eventually fails after 15--22 cycles, depending on nut size, geometry,
and test conditions. This degradation appears to be largely due to limited
domain randomization along the $z$-axis, which inadequately covers the axial drift
accumulated over many cycles. An important next step is therefore to introduce
a curriculum that progressively expands the axial variation encountered
during training, improving the long-horizon capability of the RL teacher and,
consequently, the distilled student.

To simplify the task, we deliberately initialize the nut already engaged with
the bolt. We adopted this setting after unsuccessful attempts to learn or
demonstrate nut engagement using reinforcement learning, teleoperation, and
imitation learning. We attribute much of the difficulty to the rigid nut and
the helical thread constraint, which admit only a narrow entry configuration
for successful engagement. Future work will therefore focus on autonomous nut
engagement, which we anticipate will require fine-grained fingertip-force
feedback and tactile sensing.
